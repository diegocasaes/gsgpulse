Iran-Israel War Heads Toward a Ceasefire MOU as Hormuz Disruption Reshapes Global South Energy and Tech Exposure
The Iran-Israel war, underway since February 28, 2026, is approaching a negotiated off-ramp: a ceasefire memorandum of understanding is scheduled for signature on June 19, the day after this edition closes. The war has already produced one outcome with few precedents in modern great-power conflict — the killing of Iran's Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei, with his son installed as successor — alongside repeated strikes on Strait of Hormuz shipping that, at peak disruption, cut transit through the strait by an estimated 95 to 99 percent. Iran's retaliatory strikes also hit cloud and data-center infrastructure in Bahrain and the UAE used by AWS, Microsoft, IBM, Oracle, Google, and Palantir, turning a regional war into a live stress test of Gulf-hosted digital infrastructure that much of the Global South now depends on for cloud services, payments rails, and AI compute.
The structural stakes extend well past MENA. Roughly a fifth of global oil and a significant share of LNG transits Hormuz; the disruption has driven energy-importing Global South states from the Philippines to South Africa to scramble for alternative supply and hedge against price shocks, accelerating Southeast Asia's energy-security cooperation agenda inside ASEAN. It has also intensified African and Latin American courtship of Gulf capital and security guarantees, as Gulf states themselves diversify outward in response to their own vulnerability. And it has forced a reckoning inside the tech sector — and by extension, every Global South government building digital public infrastructure or AI capacity on hyperscaler cloud — about geographic concentration risk in data-center siting. Connects to: Southeast Asia's multi-vector energy diplomacy, Africa-Gulf capital flows, Global South AI/digital sovereignty, and the broader pattern of energy-importing developing states absorbing the costs of conflicts they have no part in starting.
Why this story won: 5/5 criteria hit. The war is cross-cited across conflict, energy, finance, and tech-infrastructure coverage simultaneously; a ceasefire MOU signing on June 19 makes this maximally time-urgent; the killing of Iran's Supreme Leader is a hard-to-reverse structural shift in the regional order; and its shockwaves reach MENA, Southeast Asia, Africa, and global energy/tech markets that ripple into Latin America — clearing the geographic-multiplier bar decisively.

Brazil High
Section 301 Standoff Holds at July 15 Deadline as Negotiation and Retaliation Tracks Run in Parallel
The US-Brazil tariff dispute remains unresolved heading into the July 15 compliance deadline and July 6 public hearing. Brazil continues pursuing negotiation while keeping mirror-tariff legislation in reserve. Gulf / Africa / Latin America High
Gulf Capital Diversifies Outward as Hormuz Risk Reshapes South-South Investment Flows
As Gulf states absorb the costs of concentrated exposure to the Hormuz corridor, sovereign capital and logistics actors are deepening engagement with Africa and Latin America — both as alternative investment destinations and as hedges against MENA-zone risk. This accelerates a South-South capital reallocation already underway through infrastructure and ports investment.
Why It Matters
Global South states positioned to absorb Gulf capital diversification gain leverage they did not have to actively negotiate for — but only if they move quickly enough to shape terms before the window driven by acute Gulf risk-aversion closes.

DRC High
Cobalt Export-Quota and Revenue Audit Findings Keep Governance Crisis Live
Following the June revenue-leakage audit into the gap between record cobalt export volumes and government revenue, the DRC government is weighing quota adjustments. Western policy and media actors continue using the findings to amplify "Chinese supply chain risk" narratives around Congolese cobalt.
Why It Matters
The DRC holds 74% of global cobalt mine production. Whether the audit becomes a genuine renegotiation of who captures mineral rents, or simply ammunition for a Western realignment narrative, will shape the template for mineral-rich Global South states navigating similar pressure.

South Africa Medium
JETP Pledged-Versus-Mobilized Gap Becomes a Public Accountability Question: $8.5B Against $13.5B
International pledges for South Africa's Just Energy Transition Investment Plan stand at $13.5B, but actually mobilized finance is reported at $8.5B. The gap is now a focal point for scrutiny, alongside continuing questions about whether benefits are reaching coal-dependent communities in Mpumalanga and Limpopo.
Why It Matters
South Africa's JETP is the reference case for every future just-transition partnership in the Global South. A visible pledged-versus-delivered gap, on top of unresolved community-benefit questions, undermines the credibility of the entire JETP model as a finance mechanism — not just this one deal.

Pakistan Medium
World Bank Reclassifies Pakistan Into MENAAP Region Amid Indus Waters Treaty Tension
The World Bank's reclassification of Pakistan into its Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan (MENAAP) region marks an institutional repositioning of Pakistan's regional economic identity. It comes as the status of the Indus Waters Treaty with India remains unresolved, with downstream implications for water-security politics in South Asia.
Why It Matters
Institutional reclassification is rarely neutral bookkeeping — it signals how multilateral institutions read shifting regional alignments. Combined with unresolved Indus Waters tension, this is a marker of South Asia's quiet reorientation amid broader MENA volatility.

Pacific / Global High
Pacific Sharpens COP31 Framing: Türkiye Presides, Australia Leads Negotiations
Pacific advocacy ahead of COP31 (Antalya, Türkiye, Nov 9–20) is clarifying the dual arrangement brokered at COP30 in Belém: Türkiye holds the formal Presidency and hosts, while Australia's Minister Chris Bowen leads the negotiations track as "President of Negotiations." The Pacific Resilience Facility, the first dedicated regional climate finance mechanism, is now operative.
Why It Matters
Getting the Türkiye-Australia split right matters for how Pacific advocacy targets its lobbying — presidency and negotiations leadership are different levers, and conflating them (as shorthand often does) risks misdirected pressure on the wrong actor at the wrong moment.

Emerging Strategic Patterns

01
Cross-regional synthesis
Conflict Cost-Shifting: Energy Importers Absorb a War They Did Not Start
The Iran-Israel war's Hormuz disruption is being paid for, in real terms, by energy-importing Global South states with no stake in the conflict — through price shocks, supply scrambles, and accelerated (often costly) energy-security cooperation. This is the structural pattern behind nearly every major-power conflict of the last decade: the Global South absorbs externalized costs it had no vote in creating.

Cross-regional synthesis
Gulf Risk-Aversion as a South-South Capital Opportunity
Gulf capital's outward diversification — a direct response to its own conflict-zone exposure — is creating a real, if narrow, window for African and Latin American states to capture investment and logistics partnerships they would not otherwise have on these terms. The states that move fastest to shape terms, rather than simply accept capital, will determine whether this becomes genuine leverage or another extraction relationship with a different flag.

Pledged-Versus-Mobilized Gaps Are Becoming the JETP Model's Defining Liability
South Africa's JETP gap — $13.5B pledged against $8.5B actually mobilized — is not a one-off accounting discrepancy. It is becoming the structural liability that defines the entire just-transition-partnership model: pledges generate headlines and political capital at announcement, but delivery consistently lags, and the gap lands hardest on the coal-dependent communities the model claims to protect.

Infrastructure Concentration Risk Becomes a Live Sovereignty Argument
The Hormuz-zone data-center strikes converted an abstract digital-sovereignty argument into a concrete, demonstrated risk: critical cloud and AI infrastructure concentrated in a conflict-exposed region can be knocked offline by events entirely outside any user government's control. This materially strengthens BRICS-aligned arguments for distributed, sovereignty-respecting digital infrastructure — not as ideology, but as risk management.
