The Hormuz shock is rewiring Global South alignment in real time — and BRICS just failed its first real test

Since Israeli and US strikes on Iran on 28 February killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and triggered a succession crisis, Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz has produced a 95% collapse in crude shipping and a 99% collapse in LNG shipping through the Gulf, according to WTO trade data. The shock is no longer a Middle East story — it is now the organizing fact for Global South diplomacy. Pakistan's security architecture is fraying as Iran-border instability feeds smuggling networks and record Balochistan violence; ASEAN leaders used their May summit to frame the war as the trigger for accelerating fuel-sharing and joint power-grid initiatives, explicitly hedging against Gulf supply risk; and Foreign Policy's reporting on whether the war is "pushing Southeast Asia into China's arms" captures a wider pattern — energy insecurity is accelerating exactly the multi-alignment behavior that defines what analysts are calling "Global South 2.0."

The structural tell is what just failed at BRICS. With India chairing the bloc through 2026, member states could not produce a joint statement on the Iran war — an outcome India's own Ministry of External Affairs has acknowledged as "an exercise in futility." That failure matters more than any single statement would have: it shows that the platform Global South states have built specifically to coordinate strategic autonomy cannot yet absorb a live shock of this magnitude without fracturing along the fault line that has always run through it — Gulf-dependent members versus members for whom Iran is a security partner. The same dynamic is rippling into resource and finance politics: Gulf states (UAE, Saudi Arabia) are racing to lock in AI and compute investment partly to diversify away from a security file they no longer fully control, African and Latin American net fuel importers are absorbing a fresh terms-of-trade shock on top of an already acute debt-servicing crisis, and the Strait closure is the kind of event that will be cited for years as the moment multipolar hedging stopped being theoretical. It connects directly to the debt and minerals stories below: every regional bloc now managing this shock is the same set of states being told by the IMF that financing terms, not access, are the real fight.

Why this story won: it is the only development this cycle independently cited across MENA, South Asia, and Southeast Asia reporting, it is actively reshaping decisions inside other top stories (ASEAN energy policy, BRICS cohesion, Gulf AI strategy), the effects are accelerating in real time rather than settling, the succession crisis in Tehran makes the shock structurally hard to reverse on any short timeline, and it now touches at least three Global South regions simultaneously.

BrazilHigh

Deforestation data becomes the front line of a trade dispute

Brazil reported May Amazon deforestation 61.4% below 2025 levels, the lowest May figure on record, days after the Trump administration proposed 25% tariffs partly justified by deforestation concerns. Lula rejected the framing directly, saying Washington "doesn't understand" Brazil's path to zero deforestation by 2030.

Why it matters: climate metrics are now explicitly entering unrelated trade disputes — a precedent CECG should track for other Global South exporters facing similar tariff logic.

DRCHigh

Tshisekedi's cobalt audit tests whether resource wealth can be captured at home

An audit ordered after cabinet analysis found record export volumes weren't translating into proportional revenue delivered initial findings around June 15. The new Mining Code requires 60–100% repatriation of export revenue depending on investment-recovery phase, and a new sovereign fund (FIS RDC, March 2026) is meant to channel earnings into development rather than debt service.

Why it matters: this is the clearest current test case of whether African mineral exporters can convert volume into fiscal sovereignty rather than just feeding global supply chains.

KenyaMed

Ruto signals Africa is done negotiating minerals on Western terms alone

At the G7 summit in Lake Geneva, Ruto made clear the terms of engagement on critical minerals are shifting, alongside a separate Kenya-US minerals deal reshaping local processing and mining terms. Africa holds an estimated 30% of global critical minerals but captures only ~40% of potential revenue.

Why it matters: this is South-South leverage-building in real time — worth watching as a template other mineral-rich Global South states may follow.

PakistanHigh

Balochistan violence and Indus water rhetoric escalate together

Separatist violence in Balochistan hit new highs in May, including one of the deadliest attacks in the provincial capital in years, compounded by instability bleeding across the Iran border. Simultaneously, India's water minister has vowed to ensure "not a single drop" flows to Pakistan under the Indus Waters Treaty.

Why it matters: two structural pressure points — internal security and transboundary water — are compounding at the same moment, raising regional escalation risk.

Vietnam-Philippines ties deepen as the South China Sea dispute hits its 10th post-ruling year

Vietnam's To Lam visited Manila May 31–June 1 to mark 50 years of diplomatic relations, discussing trade, security and maritime cooperation. Why it matters: bilateral hedging between two claimant states signals ASEAN coordination on the South China Sea may be shifting from collective to minilateral.

Pacific IslandsHigh

Locked-in sea-level rise raises the stakes for COP31

NASA's sea level change science team confirms Tuvalu, Kiribati and Fiji face at least 15cm of rise over the next 30 years regardless of future emissions trajectories. Roughly 90 Pacific representatives met in May on climate justice ahead of a Pacific-based COP31 pre-COP under the Türkiye/Australia presidency arrangement.

Why it matters: this is the scientific floor under every Pacific adaptation-finance argument heading into COP31 — irreversibility strengthens, rather than weakens, the moral and legal case.

Global SouthHigh

UNCTAD quantifies the fiscal squeeze behind every regional debt fight

A June 15 UNCTAD report finds 99 developing countries home to 5.5 billion people saw rising interest payments crowd out development spending between 2018–2024; developing countries paid $384bn in interest in 2024 alone, and could save roughly $500bn a year if borrowing costs matched developed-economy rates.

Why it matters: this is the empirical backbone behind Africa's shift at the Spring Meetings from "give us access" to "give us fair terms" — and a number CECG can cite directly in finance-reform advocacy.

GlobalMed

FPIC remains invoked everywhere and enforced almost nowhere

OHCHR has opened a formal call for input on demarcation, registration and titling of Indigenous lands even as less than 43% of the 4.8 billion hectares known to be held by Indigenous Peoples and local communities is formally recognized. Carbon-offset and conservation projects in Kenya and elsewhere continue to displace pastoralist and Indigenous land use under green branding.

Why it matters: every climate and nature framework Diego tracks (NbS, JETPs, biodiversity finance) runs into the same unresolved tenure gap — this OHCHR process is a concrete advocacy hook.
