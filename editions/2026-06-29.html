Global South Utilities Institutionalize South-South Power Cooperation as WEF Confirms the Energy Transition Is Fragmenting
On June 26, China Southern Power Grid convened the Global South Power Partnership Development Forum in Hong Kong with nearly 40 utility enterprises from across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, framing Global South utilities as a coordinating bloc in cross-border grid integration, technology transfer, and financing — explicitly positioned as "an important force" in the energy transition rather than recipients of it. The forum lands eight days after the World Economic Forum released its Energy Transition Index 2026, which found that despite record global investment of $3.3 trillion (including $2.3 trillion in clean energy), geopolitical tension, supply disruption, and rising demand are driving fragmentation across the global energy landscape. Read together, the two events describe the same fork: as Western-anchored transition architecture splinters under tariff disputes, conflict spillover, and reshoring pressure, a parallel Global South-coordinated track is forming around grid interconnection and utility-to-utility partnership rather than waiting for multilateral finance to catch up.
The structural stakes are about who writes the next decade's energy-infrastructure rules. If utility-led South-South cooperation matures into actual cross-border grid interconnection — fuel-sharing arrangements of the kind ASEAN framed at its May summit, lithium-and-grid deals like Russia's with Bolivia, or the financing flows UNCTAD tracks moving into African mineral-to-power value chains — it builds an alternative dependency structure that doesn't run through Bretton Woods institutions or G7 climate-finance pledges. That is a hedge against exactly the kind of debt-service squeeze and JETP delivery shortfall (pledged $13.5B against $8.5B mobilized in South Africa's case) that keeps recurring in this scan. It also reframes the G7's Evian outreach to Brazil, India, and Kenya — explicitly pitched as "shifting from aid to investment" — as defensive positioning against a Global South energy bloc that no longer needs an invitation to the table. Connects to: Africa's debt-distress and JETP-gap signals, the DRC mineral-rents fight, BRICS diplomatic coordination under India's chairship, and the G7's critical-minerals outreach to Global South leaders.
Why this story won: 5/5 criteria hit. It is independently confirmed across multilateral data (WEF), state-aligned and business press coverage of the Hong Kong forum, and UNCTAD's parallel minerals-trade reporting; it actively recontextualizes the debt, JETP, and G7-outreach stories elsewhere in this edition; both component events fall within or just before the scan window; it marks a durable institutional shift — utility-to-utility infrastructure commitments are far stickier than pledges — and it spans Africa, Asia, and Latin America simultaneously by design.

Pakistan / India High
Indus Waters Treaty Crisis Escalates to War-Risk Rhetoric as Kashmir Unrest Turns Deadly
Pakistan's Defense Minister warned on June 19 that water-security threats from India's treaty suspension could trigger war, even as he said current conditions don't yet warrant it. Days later, Pakistan-administered Kashmir suffered its deadliest unrest in years — at least 24 killed in protests, with roads shut and internet blocked.
Why It Matters
A bilateral water-sharing treaty that survived three wars is now being discussed in war-risk terms — a structurally unprecedented escalation that signals how thin the guardrails around South Asian de-escalation have become, and how quickly resource disputes can convert into security crises.

Tehran's June 26 rejection of a joint Gulf-US statement on Strait of Hormuz security came even as a US-Iran memorandum of understanding — including a proposed $300 billion investment fund — advances toward formal signature in Switzerland as part of a broader war-settlement package.

DRC High
Mineral-Rents Standoff Persists: Zero Compliance with Worker-Equity Rule Before July Deadline
President Tshisekedi's revenue-leakage audit identifies structural failures in repatriation compliance and joint-venture governance, while not a single mining company has achieved formal compliance with the 5% worker-equity rule despite eight years on the books. A new FIS RDC sovereign fund and forfeiture-based cobalt-quota system signal the state is trying to tighten its grip regardless.
Why It Matters
The DRC supplies roughly three-quarters of global cobalt. Whether the state can convert export volume into captured rents — through equity rules, sovereign-fund channeling, or quota forfeiture — is the clearest live test of resource nationalism's staying power against organized capital pushback.
